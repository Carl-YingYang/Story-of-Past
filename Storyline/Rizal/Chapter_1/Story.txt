CHAPTER 1 — "Green Fields, Broken Fences"

Historical Period: Prologue (present day) → 1861–1872, Calamba, Laguna

Setting: A present-day bedroom/study desk (prologue only); then the Rizal-Mercado household in Calamba, Laguna — a bahay na bato near the town plaza, and the surrounding Dominican-leased agricultural estate.

Historical Purpose: Establishes Rizal's origins in relative privilege as an inquilino family, and the formative economic and personal injustices — the Calamba rent dispute, his mother's education of him, his mother's wrongful imprisonment, and the death of his infant sister Concepcion — that planted the earliest seeds of his critical worldview.

Narrative Summary: The chapter opens with the present-day student falling asleep over their Rizal project, then waking disoriented in the body of young José in the family home in Calamba. The player, as a child, learns letters and numbers from Teodora Alonso, explores the estate, and gradually witnesses — rather than has explained to them — the tension between the Mercado family and the Dominican estate administrators over land rent. The emotional low point is the memory/experience of the family's hardships: Teodora's unjust imprisonment on a fabricated charge, and the earlier death of infant Concepcion, Rizal's first documented experience of grief.

Key Historical Events:

Birth in Calamba, June 19, 1861, to an affluent inquilino family. [DOCUMENTED FACT]
Baptism June 22, 1861. [DOCUMENTED FACT]
Teodora Alonso as his first teacher, introducing literature and mathematics. [DOCUMENTED FACT]
The Dominican estate's arbitrary rent increase, Francisco Rizal's agrarian protest, and the family's eviction and destroyed homes. [DOCUMENTED FACT]
Denial of Christian burial to a brother-in-law (Mariano Herbosa) tied to friar reprisal — note: this occurred later (Herbosa's death), but its roots lie in this same friar-family conflict; presented here as a documented later consequence foreshadowed, not as occurring in childhood. [DOCUMENTED FACT — but chronologically later; used as foreshadowing dialogue/reference, not as an in-chapter event]
Teodora Alonso's wrongful imprisonment by colonial authorities over a petty dispute. [DOCUMENTED FACT]
Death of infant sister Concepcion (1862–1865), recorded by Rizal as his first profound experience of grief. [DOCUMENTED FACT]
Family surname "Rizal" adopted following the 1849 Clavería decree. [DOCUMENTED FACT]

Important Characters:

Teodora Alonso Realonda — Mother; highly educated, his first teacher. Documented as central to his early intellectual formation and as a victim of colonial judicial injustice.
Francisco Mercado — Father; led the agrarian protest against the Dominican estate, shaping José's early anti-clerical, anti-injustice instincts.
Paciano Rizal — Older brother, present in the household during this period; historically documented as José's later mentor, introduced here as an early formative presence.
Saturnina, Narcisa, Olimpia, Lucia, Maria (elder sisters) — Documented as part of the large sibling household; used for atmosphere and family texture, not invented dialogue.

Player Role: Explore the family home and estate grounds; complete simple lesson-based mini-interactions with Teodora (reading, counting); listen in on (not confront) conversations between Francisco and the estate administrators; find the family's land-lease ledger as a discoverable document; experience the loss of Concepcion as a quiet, understated emotional beat.

Gameplay Highlights:

A gentle "learning to read" interactive sequence with Teodora.
Exploration of the Calamba estate, finding environmental storytelling details (rent notices, boundary markers).
A discoverable object: the family's actual land-lease documents (representing the historical rent dispute).
A slow-paced, wordless vignette depicting Concepcion's death, framed through the household's mourning rather than melodrama.

Major Emotional/Narrative Moment: The quiet grief following Concepcion's death — Rizal's first recorded encounter with loss, presented without invented dialogue, through household ritual and silence.

Important Historical Discovery: The player should understand that Rizal's convictions did not begin in Europe — they began at home, in the concrete economic injustice of tenant farming under friar estates, and in his mother's imprisonment, which exposed him early to a corrupt colonial justice system.

Chapter Ending/Transition: As José grows older, his father and brother Paciano decide he needs a more rigorous education. The chapter ends with young José preparing to leave for Biñan and then Manila — the first of many departures that will define his life.

Framing device: In the present day, a Filipino high school student — call them the Player in-game (name left customizable) — is up late finishing a school project on José Rizal. Surrounded by photocopied textbook pages, a dog-eared copy of the Noli, and a half-written reflection paper, the student knows Rizal only as "the guy on the peso coin who wrote two long novels and got shot." Tired, unmotivated, and slightly resentful of the assignment, the student falls asleep at the table.

They wake up as José Rizal himself — not replacing him in a fantasy sense, but inhabiting his documented life, moving through real remembered moments in first person. The game does not ask "what if history were different?" The game asks: what does it feel like to actually live the choices, losses, and convictions that the textbook reduces to a paragraph?

Themes:

The distance between knowing about someone and understanding them.
How ordinary family injustice (the Calamba land dispute, Teodora's imprisonment) can radicalize a mind, one grievance at a time.
The slow, documented evolution from assimilationist reformer to disillusioned realist — not a single dramatic conversion, but years of accumulated evidence.
Intellect as resistance: Rizal's weapons are books, scalpels, surveying tools, and a printing press — not guns.
The cost of conviction: exile, separation from family, the abandonment of a stable medical career, the refusal to endorse a war he believed would fail.

Player experience: Exploration-first, conversation-driven, document-and-object-based discovery. The player reconstructs understanding the way a historian does — by finding letters, overhearing conversations, examining rent ledgers, reading annotated manuscripts, and observing (not fighting through) the political tension of colonial Philippines and Restoration-era Europe. Historical outcomes are fixed; the player's agency is in how thoroughly they understand why those outcomes happened.
